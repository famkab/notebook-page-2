European Digital Education Hub

Two squads with the European Commission’s Digital Education Hub, across a year. One asked how data can inform the design of learning spaces. The other asked how public–private partnerships shape AI adoption in education across Europe.

My role Dublin · Brussels · online · 2025
Role Research Collaborator
Led on I wrote the section on data-informed design through learning and space analytics for the Future Classrooms report, and contributed to the framework on AI adoption in education.
Team Educators, researchers, designers, policymakers and technologists from across Europe, working in small sub-groups.
How it ran

A workshop, then five months of squad work

The Hub selects participants for a face-to-face workshop that opens the topic, then an online squad that carries it for several months. I was selected twice.

Squad oneFuture Classrooms

Workshop in Dublin, 23–24 July 2025, then an online squad from August to December. How new technologies are used in learning spaces: physical, digital and hybrid ones.

Squad twoAI Adoption in Europe

Meeting in Brussels, with the squad running online around it. How public–private partnerships shape the adoption of AI in education.

Squad one

Learning and space analytics

Dublin opened with a study visit to Trinity College Dublin, then empathy mapping and scenario design, with the group working through learning analytics, emotional wellbeing, and what it means to design for the people who use a space rather than the people who commission it. The squad took that into five months of sub-group work, and I sat in the one on data-informed design.

The sub-group’s question was how data can help design smarter and more responsive learning environments, and what it should not be used for. My part was the section on learning and space analytics: what a space can reasonably tell you about learning, what it cannot, and where the line sits between designing with data and surveilling the people in the room.

  • The report“Empowering Data-Informed Design through Learning and Space Analytics”, drafted with the sub-group. I wrote the analytics section.
  • Metric cardsA set of cards for digital learning spaces, so a school or institution can pick what it will measure before it buys anything that measures.
  • The constraintEverything had to hold across very different national systems, budgets and starting points, which is what stops a framework from being written for one country.
Squad two

Public–private partnerships and AI adoption

The second squad looked at how public–private partnerships shape AI adoption in education across Europe. Meeting in Brussels made it clear the problem was harder than the group had assumed: the arrangements are opaque, they vary by country, and the people they affect most have the least visibility of them.

What the group built was a set of game-like cards: a way to walk someone through a partnership and make the policy space legible to the people who have to live with it. It started as a rough brainstorm and ended as something practical enough to use in a room.

Cards were the right form for the same reason they were the right form for Beeline Unplugged. An abstract arrangement becomes arguable once it is an object on a table that several people can move around.

What it changed

Where it went next

The space analytics work taught me how much physical space matters to learning analytics, which is not obvious from inside digital product design. That is what led directly to designing offline, tactile discovery toolkits that bridge a physical room and a digital service.

Both squads were rooms where nobody shared my background, and the work had to hold across ten or more national systems at once. Writing for that audience is a different discipline from writing for one organisation, and it is the reason the Beeline metrics framework is written to be read by a delivery team, a programme board and a community partner in the same way.
