How I work

where it starts

Curiosity is where most of my ideas start, and a healthy obsession with systems thinking is what turns them into something useful. I run a book club that reads outside what I would choose myself, I hike, and I play basketball with people who work in completely different fields. Each of those rooms hands me a perspective I would not have met inside my own field, picked up from people who think differently.

learning

My training crossed domains long before my career did, and reflects the same curiosity. I read Pharmacology and Physiology at the University of Manchester, trained as a science teacher in London, completed an MSc in education technology at the Université Côte d’Azur in Nice, and certified in data science in Barcelona. Along the way I interned at the Innovation Centre for Entrepreneurship and at the Italian Institute of Technology (IIT) where I learned all about service and product design.

global

The four meet in the work I do now. I lead the design of Beeline, the young person’s version of the MBacc and now the national blueprint for talking to young people through digital services, across all ten Greater Manchester authorities, and write its metrics framework myself. The European Commission’s Digital Education Hub selected me twice to bring that combination to its work: I wrote the section on data-informed design through learning and space analytics for the Future Classrooms 2040 report, and contributed to its framework on AI adoption in education.

both scales

The design work itself runs across very different scales, and I move between them often. I have mapped an entire regional careers system from Year 7 to Year 13 to find where young people actually lose momentum, and I have written the metrics framework that turns feeling and emotion into something we can measure, act on, and draw service design takeaways from. I run structured co-design with the young people the service is built for, using the of child participation, which builds their voice into the process from the start. That Lundy training has doubled as good practice for user research and usability testing more broadly, and it shaped how I think about centring the people we design for.

listening, then building

I turn workshop notes, stakeholder challenge, and partner feedback into a single evidence base that a delivery team, a programme board, and a community partner can all read the same way. Some weeks that means a whiteboard and a room full of people. Other weeks it means building the actual tool, a workbook, a tracker, an interactive page, that the service runs on afterwards.

reading the evidence

I go into the data myself, in Python and in SQL on BigQuery, and I read what comes back with Alex Edmans’ Ladder of Misinference: is the number accurate, is the sample representative, could something else explain it, and does it hold beyond this case? On Beeline that is how a smaller number became the truer one, once we randomised the order of the gateway cards.

practising an open mind

What matters most to me underneath all of it is staying open. A new environment changes how I work as much as what I know, and I would rather learn a method from an unfamiliar room and combine it with what I know, than try to squeeze a way of working into places that do not fit. That is the part of my toolkit that keeps growing, one challenge at a time.
